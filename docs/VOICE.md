# Writing Voice & Style Guide

A reference for writing or rewriting any content on this portfolio. Use this whenever generating, editing, or rewriting copy — posts, case studies, bios, captions, anything.

---

## The Core

**Conversational but precise.** Sounds like talking to someone smart, not presenting to a room. No performance, no posturing. Say the thing directly.

**Observation-first.** The frame is: here's what I saw, here's what I did about it. Not "I am passionate about X." Show the situation, show the response. Let the reader draw the conclusion.

**Work and life are not separate.** Personal interests (triathlon, coffee, making things) connect to how she thinks about work. Don't sanitize one out of the other. The through-line is usually: intentionality, systems, craft, grit.

---

## Tone

- Direct, grounded, a bit dry humor
- Never precious about the work. Honest about what was hard or what didn't work.
- Low temperature. No exclamation points. No "excited to share."
- Technical precision where needed, but always accessible
- Honest about trade-offs and constraints, not just wins

---

## Rhythm

Sentences should breathe and carry weight. The default is a sentence that builds — connects ideas with commas, conjunctions, or dashes — rather than stopping short. Don't split a thought across two sentences when it belongs in one.

Short sentences exist, but they're earned by context, not used as a default register. A short sentence that follows longer ones can land hard — but only if the surrounding rhythm sets it up. Don't reach for short to sound snappy or decisive.

Fragments are only for dry or sarcastic humor — never for sounding punchy, editorial, or clever. A dry aside that happens to be short is fine. "Five items. No redundancy." is not the voice.

No rhetorical questions. Ever. They read as filler and break the directness of the writing.

Short paragraphs: 2-4 sentences as a default.

---

## What to Never Do

- **Filler openers.** Never start with "As a designer...", "In today's world...", "I've always believed...", or any sentence that could be deleted without losing meaning.
- **Preamble before the point.** Don't explain why something matters before showing that it does. Start with the thing itself.
- **Wrap-up paragraphs.** If the last paragraph restates what you already said, cut it. The ending should do new work or land a final specific detail. Not a summary.
- **Claimed passion.** Never say "I'm passionate about X." If it's true, the writing already shows it.
- **Generic lessons.** If a takeaway could apply to any designer anywhere, it's not specific enough. Make it concrete to the actual situation.
- **Corporate speak.** No "leverage," "synergy," "spearhead," "drive," "enable," "align stakeholders," "world-class," "under the hood," or anything that belongs in a LinkedIn post.
- **Over-polished sentences.** Writing that sounds like it went through five rounds of editing loses the person. Keep some roughness.
- **Fluff.** Every sentence should earn its place. If removing it doesn't change the meaning, remove it.
- **Vague intensifiers.** No "very," "really," "extremely," "incredibly."
- **Em dashes overused.** Max one or two per piece. Before using one, try: a comma, a colon, a semicolon, or splitting into two sentences. Colons work for elaboration ("she wasn't pleased: she was right"). Semicolons work for related clauses. Commas handle most parentheticals. Em dashes are for genuine pivots or asides that resist all other punctuation — not for joining sentences because it felt natural to reach for one.
- **False modesty or humble-bragging.**
- **Parentheticals as separate sentences.** Keep asides inside the sentence they belong to, not broken out as their own fragment. "Slow, political work (worth it)." not "Slow, political work. (Worth it.)"

---

## What to Prefer

- **Active voice.** "I built" not "I was responsible for building."
- **Specific details.** "14 button styles, 8 shades of gray" not "inconsistent UI."
- **Simple words.** "use" not "leverage," "help" not "enable," "build" not "develop solutions."
- **Examples over abstractions.** Show the thing, then name the pattern.
- **Honest framing.** Acknowledge what didn't work, what was hard, what was uncertain.

---

## Structure

- Lead with the situation or problem, not the solution
- Use headers to break up long pieces — short and specific, not clever
- Lists only for genuinely list-like things. Not as a substitute for a real sentence.
- No "In conclusion" or equivalent. End on a specific detail, a real observation, or nothing.

---

## Core Values & Themes

These should surface naturally, not be stated explicitly:

- **Human agency in the AI age.** Where do humans need to stay in the loop?
- **Standards as infrastructure.** Consistency enables connection.
- **Intentionality.** Deliberate choices about what deserves to be systematic.
- **Craft.** Quality takes time, even when AI accelerates production.
- **Fail better.** Honest about iteration and what didn't work first.

---

## Voice in Practice

**Wrong:**
> As someone who cares deeply about design systems, I've learned that consistency is at the heart of great product experiences. This project taught me the importance of collaboration and communication.

**Right:**
> The system was inconsistent. Fourteen button styles, eight grays, no agreement on anything. The audit took three weeks. Then the real work started.

---

**Wrong:**
> I'm passionate about bridging the gap between design and engineering to create more cohesive and scalable digital products.

**Right:**
> Designers were using Claude. The outputs were generic. Not wrong, just disconnected from what we'd actually built. That gap was worth closing.
